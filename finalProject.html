Background

Predicting house prices aids in real estimation of homes, approximation of net equity for particular individuals, and areas of prospective relocation for buyers. Statistical modelling helps us understand housing markets by observing which attributes (e.g. age, area, number of rooms, etc) are able to best predict a house price. It also helps us determine which attributes should be included and excluded when predicting a house price based on their correlation to the target variable.

Literature Review

Housing prices are influenced by many different factors, including characteristics of the property itself, location, and changes in the overall housing market. Since multiple factors can affect house prices at the same time, previous research has commonly used regression methods to understand the relationship between these variables. Multiple regression models are useful in housing research because they allow researchers to estimate the effect of individual factors. This approach helps provide a better understanding of which features of a property have the strongest relationship with its selling price.

Morel (2022) examined changes in housing prices across major Canadian cities and analyzed how structural and neighbourhood characteristics influenced property values. The study considered factors such as house size, age, and location, including the distance of properties from downtown areas. One of the main findings was that during the COVID-19 pandemic, the price advantage of homes located closer to downtown areas decreased. As remote work became more common, many buyers started to prefer larger suburban homes with more space rather than smaller properties in urban locations. This study demonstrates that housing prices are affected by more than just physical characteristics of a house, as market conditions and changes in buyer preferences can also influence the importance of different predictors. It also supports the use of regression models because they allow researchers to separate the effects of different housing and location factors.

Similarly, Schaefer and Panagiotoglou (2024) used a hedonic pricing model to analyze housing prices in Montreal. Hedonic pricing models are commonly used in real estate research because they estimate how different characteristics contribute to the overall value of a property. Their study included internal housing features, such as bedrooms, bathrooms, and unit size, along with external factors related to neighbourhood characteristics. By including multiple variables in their model, the researchers were able to measure the effect of a specific location-based factor while keeping other housing characteristics constant. This approach is closely related to the method used in this project, where multiple regression is used to determine the individual effects of variables such as Overall Quality, Gr Liv Area, and Neighborhood on SalePrice.

Another important source for this project is De Cock (2011), which introduced the Ames Housing dataset used in this analysis. The paper describes the dataset as an alternative to the Boston Housing dataset and provides detailed information about residential properties in Ames, Iowa. The dataset includes many variables related to housing characteristics, including living area, basement size, garage features, construction quality, year built, and neighbourhood information. While De Cock (2011) does not directly investigate which factors affect housing prices, it provides a valuable dataset for applying regression techniques. The large number of available predictors makes the dataset useful for studying how different property characteristics are associated with variations in sale prices.

Overall, previous research shows that factors such as house size, quality, and location are important predictors of housing prices. Studies have consistently found that structural characteristics and neighbourhood differences can significantly affect property values. These findings support the selection of variables used in this project, including Overall Quality, Gr Liv Area, Total Basement SF, Garage characteristics, Year Built, and Neighborhood. By applying multiple regression analysis to the Ames Housing dataset, this project builds on previous research by examining which factors remain significant when multiple housing characteristics are considered together.

Discussion

Key Findings

  • Overall Qual: strong positive effect, p < 0.001. Each one-unit increase is associated with an estimated 11.65% increase in expected sale price – the strongest predictor in the model.
  • Gr Liv Area: p < 0.001, +0.03% per additional square foot, which compounds to a meaningful price difference over hundreds of square feet.
  • Year Built: significant, +0.25% per year more recent (p < 0.001).
  • Total Bsmt SF (+0.01%/sq ft, p < 0.001) and Garage Cars (+5.64%/car, p < 0.001) both significant; Garage Area significant but weaker (+0.01%/sq ft, p = 0.020).
  • Full Bath (p = 0.157), TotRms AbvGrd (p = 0.492), and Bedroom AbvGr (p = 0.496) were not significant, likely because they’re redundant with Gr.Liv.Area (see VIF/correlation results above).
  • Neighborhood was also not statistically significant (p = 0.618) in this version of the model. This is a direct consequence of how it’s encoded here: each neighborhood was assigned an arbitrary numeric code (0-27), which forces the model to assume neighborhoods have a fixed, linear ranking and that moving “up” by one code always changes price by the same amount, regardless of which two neighborhoods are being compared. Since that ordering doesn’t reflect anything real about the neighborhoods, the true (and, as seen in earlier exploration, often large) location-based price differences get averaged out into a single near-zero slope. This is a limitation of the numeric encoding, not evidence that location doesn’t matter.

Direction of Effects

All significant coefficients had signs matching economic intuition – quality, size, basement area, garage capacity, and more recent construction all raised price, with no implausible effects. Neighborhood’s near-zero, non-significant coefficient is not economically implausible either, but reflects the limitation of its numeric encoding rather than a real absence of location effects.

Practical Interpretation

Overall quality and living area matter most for estimating a home’s value in Ames, with basement size, garage capacity, and year built as secondary factors. Bedroom/bathroom counts added little once size was already in the model. Neighborhood’s apparent lack of significance here should not be read as “location doesn’t matter” – it is an artifact of treating neighborhood names as if they were ordered numbers, which is a modeling limitation rather than a market fact.

Expected vs. Actual Results

Overall Qual and Gr Liv Area dominating the model matched our expectations from the literature review. The complete drop-out of Full Bath, Bedroom AbvGr, and TotRms AbvGrd as non-significant was more surprising. The most surprising result was Neighborhood coming out non-significant, since location is consistently found to matter in the housing literature (Morel, 2022; Schaefer & Panagiotoglou, 2024) – this pushed us to recognize that the issue was our encoding choice (numeric codes) rather than a genuine absence of a location effect.

Conclusion

This project used multiple linear regression to model house sale prices (log-transformed) in Ames, Iowa using overall quality, above-ground living area, total basement square footage, garage capacity, garage area, year built, full bathrooms, bedrooms above grade, total rooms above grade, and neighborhood (encoded numerically). The model achieved R² = 0.822 (Adjusted R² = 0.822), explaining about 82.2% of the variation in log sale price, with a highly significant overall F-test (F = 1349.9 on 10 and 2917 df, p < 2.2e-16). Overall Qual, Gr Liv Area, Year Built, Total Bsmt SF, and Garage Cars/Area were all significant predictors, consistent with the multi-attribute regression approach supported by Morel (2022) and Schaefer and Panagiotoglou (2024), while Full Bath, Bedroom AbvGr, TotRms AbvGrd, and – notably – Neighborhood were not significant in this version of the model.

Limitations

Only 10 of 80+ available variables were used; some relevant characteristics (lot size, exterior condition, remodel history) were excluded. A more significant limitation is the numeric encoding of Neighborhood: assigning each neighborhood an arbitrary code from 0-27 forces the regression to treat location as if it were a single continuous, linearly-ordered variable, which is not a valid assumption for a categorical attribute with no natural ordering. This is almost certainly why Neighborhood came out statistically non-significant here (p = 0.618) despite location being a well-documented driver of house prices in the broader literature (Morel, 2022; Schaefer & Panagiotoglou, 2024) – the true, likely large effects of individual neighborhoods are being averaged into a single near-zero slope. VIF showed moderate correlation among Garage.Cars, Garage.Area, and Gr.Liv.Area, and residuals showed a few low-priced outliers violating normality in the tails – both are additional reasons to interpret individual coefficients with some caution.

Future Research

Future work could add more predictors (lot area, exterior quality, remodel age), test neighborhood-by-quality interactions, investigate the flagged outlier sales, or compare against nonlinear/ML approaches, similar to the hedonic modeling frameworks used by Morel (2022) and Schaefer and Panagiotoglou (2024). Given the concerns raised above, re-encoding Neighborhood as a categorical/dummy variable rather than a numeric code would also be a natural next step, since it would let each neighborhood have its own independent effect on price rather than forcing them onto a single arbitrary numeric scale.
