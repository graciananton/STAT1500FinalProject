Background

Predicting house prices aids in real estimation of homes, approximation of net equity for particular individuals, and areas of prospective relocation for buyers. Statistical modelling helps us understand housing markets by observing which attributes (e.g. age, area, number of rooms, etc) are able to best predict a house price. It also helps us determine which attributes should be included and excluded when predicting a house price based on their correlation to the target variable.

Literature Review

Sources used for this project:

  • Morel, L. (2022). Analyzing the house price boom in the suburbs of Canada’s major cities during the pandemic. Bank of Canada Staff Analytical Note, No. 2022-7. https://www.bankofcanada.ca/2022/06/staff-analytical-note-2022-7/ – a recent Canadian study that regresses house prices on structural and neighbourhood characteristics (including distance from downtown) across major Canadian cities, finding that the price premium for downtown proximity narrowed sharply during the pandemic as buyers shifted toward larger homes in the suburbs. Supports using a multi-attribute regression approach to separate the effect of physical house characteristics from location.
  • Schaefer, M., & Panagiotoglou, D. (2024). Evaluating the effects of supervised consumption sites on housing prices in Montreal, Canada using interrupted time series and hedonic price models. Drug and Alcohol Dependence Reports, 11, 100242. https://doi.org/10.1016/j.dadr.2024.100242 – a recent Canadian application of hedonic price modeling, adjusting for internal attributes (bedrooms, bathrooms, unit size) and external/neighbourhood attributes to isolate the effect of a single locational factor on price. Demonstrates the same core logic used in this project: holding structural characteristics constant to estimate the independent effect of a given predictor.
  • De Cock, D. (2011). Ames, Iowa: Alternative to the Boston Housing Data as an End of Semester Regression Project. Journal of Statistics Education, 19(3). https://jse.amstat.org/v19n3/decock.pdf – the methodological source for the Ames Housing dataset used in this project.

Discussion

Key Findings

  • Overall Qual: strong positive effect, p < 0.001. Each one-unit increase is associated with an estimated 10.68% increase in expected sale price – the strongest predictor in the model.
  • Gr Liv Area: p < 0.001, +0.02% per additional square foot, which compounds to a meaningful price difference over hundreds of square feet.
  • Total Bsmt SF (+0.01%/sq ft, p < 0.001) and Garage Cars (+4.12%/car, p < 0.001) both significant; Garage Area significant but weaker (+0.01%/sq ft, p = 0.005).
  • Year Built: significant, +0.18% per year more recent (p < 0.001).
  • Neighborhood: significant as a group (ANOVA p < 0.001). Crawford, Clear Creek, Northridge Heights, Green Hill, Stone Brook, Northridge, Timber, and Veenker show significant premiums (roughly 13-66%); Brookside Dale, IDOTRR, and Meadow Village show significant discounts (roughly 13-18%).
  • Full Bath (p = 0.74), Bedroom AbvGr (p = 0.56), and TotRms AbvGrd (p = 0.50) were not significant, likely because they’re redundant with Gr.Liv.Area (see VIF/correlation results above).

Direction of Effects

All significant coefficients had signs matching economic intuition – quality, size, basement area, garage capacity, newer construction, and better neighborhoods all raised price, with no implausible effects.

Practical Interpretation

Overall quality and living area matter most for estimating a home’s value in Ames, with basement size, garage capacity, year built, and neighborhood as secondary factors. Bedroom/bathroom counts added little once size was already in the model.

Expected vs. Actual Results

Overall Qual and Gr Liv Area dominating the model matched our expectations from the literature review. The complete drop-out of Full Bath, Bedroom AbvGr, and TotRms AbvGrd as non-significant was more surprising, as was how wide the spread of neighborhood effects turned out to be (premiums over 60%, discounts near 18%).

Conclusion

This project used multiple linear regression to model house sale prices (log-transformed) in Ames, Iowa using overall quality, above-ground living area, total basement square footage, garage capacity, garage area, year built, full bathrooms, bedrooms above grade, total rooms above grade, and neighborhood (as dummy variables). The model achieved R² = 0.849 (Adjusted R² = 0.847), explaining about 84.9% of the variation in log sale price, with a highly significant overall F-test (F = 451.6 on 36 and 2891 df, p < 2.2e-16). Overall Qual, Gr Liv Area, and Neighborhood were the most robust predictors, consistent with the multi-attribute regression approach supported by Morel (2022) and Schaefer and Panagiotoglou (2024), while Full Bath, Bedroom AbvGr, and TotRms AbvGrd were not significant.

Limitations

Only 10 of 80+ available variables were used; some relevant characteristics (lot size, exterior condition, remodel history) were excluded. Neighborhood dummy coding assumes a fixed premium/discount that doesn’t interact with other characteristics. VIF showed moderate correlation among Garage.Cars, Garage.Area, Year.Built, and Gr.Liv.Area, and residuals showed a few low-priced outliers violating normality in the tails – both are reasons to interpret individual coefficients with some caution.

Future Research

Future work could add more predictors (lot area, exterior quality, remodel age), test neighborhood-by-quality interactions, investigate the flagged outlier sales, or compare against nonlinear/ML approaches, similar to the hedonic modeling frameworks used by Morel (2022) and Schaefer and Panagiotoglou (2024).
